Where a child with a disability spends the school day is one of the most important decisions an IEP team makes. Nationally, about two in three school-age students with IEPs now spend most of the day in a general education class. In some districts, fewer than one in five do.

This article explains what the law calls the least restrictive environment (LRE), how inclusion is measured, what critical legal scholarship adds, and how to look up the figures for any U.S. school district in the IDEA Atlas. It is written for families, teachers and school leaders who want to ask better questions about where students learn.

What the law requires

The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) sets a clear starting point. Students with disabilities are to be educated with their nondisabled classmates to the maximum extent appropriate (34 CFR 300.114).

Removal from the general class is the exception. Special classes, separate schools or other removal may happen only when the nature or severity of a disability means the student cannot be educated satisfactorily in general classes, even with supplementary aids and services.

Three further rules shape every placement decision (Center for Parent Information and Resources):

  • A full range of options. Districts must offer a continuum of placements, from general classes with support to special classes, special schools, home instruction and hospital settings. Services should follow the child, rather than the child moving to the services.
  • An individual, yearly decision. Placement is decided at least once a year, based on the student's IEP, by a group that includes the parents. It is made as close to home as possible, in the school the student would otherwise attend unless the IEP requires something else.
  • The child's needs, not the system's. A placement cannot rest on what the district has available, such as staffing or budget.

In short, the law does not require every student to be in a general class all day. It requires a district to try the general class with real support first, and to justify each step away from it.

How inclusion is measured

The first figure, 80% or more of the day, is the one most often used to compare inclusion. States report it to the U.S. Department of Education each year as part of their State Performance Plans.

This figure counts minutes, not quality. A student can be in a general class all day without the support the IEP promises, and a student in a separate class for part of the day may be getting exactly what they need. The number is a starting point for questions, not a verdict on a school.

What the data show

Inclusion has grown steadily for more than three decades. In 1989, about one in three school-age students with IEPs spent most of the day in a general class. By 2022, about two in three did.

The national figure hides wide differences between places. In 2024–25, the typical district in New Jersey placed 52.1 of every 100 students with IEPs in general classes 80% or more of the day. The typical district in Utah placed 87.3. Across the country, about 1 in 11 districts reported fewer than half of their students with IEPs in general classes most of the day.

These state figures are for the typical (median) district, so small districts count as much as large ones. They describe what districts reported, not how well students are served.

What critical legal scholarship adds

Legal scholars have long argued that LRE is not a neutral technical rule. It carries choices about who counts as different, who decides, and who pays. Four lines of work help explain why the same numbers can mean very different things.

The dilemma of difference

Martha Minow (1985, 1990) showed that law faces a recurring dilemma when it responds to difference. A label such as disability opens the door to services, yet the same label can deepen stigma. Treating everyone alike ignores real needs, while treating some students differently can reproduce the very difference that disadvantaged them. Minow argued that difference is not simply a trait of the child. It arises from the norms built into institutions, such as what a “regular” classroom expects. For LRE, her question is not only where a student is placed, but what about the general classroom makes separation look necessary.

The integration presumption under debate

Ruth Colker (2006) traced IDEA’s integration presumption back to its origins and argued that Congress was right to adopt it, but that courts should apply it more flexibly, because for some children it can work against an appropriate IEP. Samuel Bagenstos and Mark Weber replied that the failures Colker described reflect schools’ refusal to provide real integration, with real services, rather than a flaw in the presumption itself. That exchange is still the clearest map of the policy argument over LRE. In Disabled Education (2013), Colker also showed how features of IDEA built in from the start limit its benefits for low-income children and children of color.

Who gets extra resources

In Jumping the Queue (1997), Mark Kelman and Gillian Lester asked how schools should share scarce resources among students who struggle, and why a disability label can bring entitlements that other struggling students do not receive. Their account drew strong criticism from disability scholars and advocates. The debate still matters, because placements and services track the resources, advocacy and information that families bring to an IEP meeting.

Race and dis/ability together

Beth Ferri and David Connor (2005) placed the overrepresentation of students of color in special education “in the shadow of Brown”, as a form of separation that persisted after school desegregation. Subini Annamma, David Connor and Beth Ferri (2013) built on this work to propose Dis/ability Critical Race Studies (DisCrit), which treats race and ability as constructed together. For LRE, DisCrit asks who ends up in the most separate settings, and whether those patterns follow race as well as disability.

Read together, this work suggests a few habits for reading inclusion data. Treat a district’s placement figures as the product of its norms and resources, not only of its students’ needs. Ask how the figures differ by race, disability category and family income, not only in total. And remember that inclusion without support does not satisfy IDEA either.

Inclusion from the bottom up

Laws and state targets set the floor. The work of inclusion happens closer to the classroom, and it often starts there too.

  • Students. Students with disabilities know which classes welcome them and which only seat them. Asking them, in ways that fit how they communicate, is the most direct measure of belonging.
  • Families. Parents are members of every placement team by law. They can also organize, through special education parent advisory councils and parent centers, to raise questions across a whole district rather than one IEP at a time.
  • Teachers. General and special educators who plan and co-teach together are often the ones who show a district that a student can succeed in a general class.
  • Schools and districts. Building leaders decide schedules, staffing and professional learning. Those choices determine whether the support written into an IEP can actually reach a general classroom.

Public data helps each of these groups. A parent council that can show its district places far fewer students in general classes than similar districts has a concrete starting point for a conversation with the school board.

Look up your own district

The IDEA Atlas has a page for every public school district in the country, in English and Spanish. Search by district name, city or ZIP code. Each page shows where students with IEPs learn, side by side with the typical district in the state.

Take Paterson Public School District in New Jersey as an example. In 2024–25, 18.5 of every 100 students with IEPs there spent 80% or more of the day in general classes. The typical New Jersey district reported 52.1. The page also shows how many students spent most of the day in separate classes or separate schools.

A figure like this does not say why. It may reflect the students a district serves, the programs it runs for neighboring towns, or how it reports. But it is a fair reason to ask how placement decisions are made.

Questions to ask

For families at an IEP meeting

  1. What supports and services were tried in the general class before a more separate setting was proposed?
  2. Which parts of the day will my child spend with classmates without disabilities, including lunch, recess and electives?
  3. Is this the school my child would attend if they did not have a disability? If not, why not?
  4. How will we know whether this placement is working, and when will we look at it again?

For school and district leaders

  1. How does our share of students in general classes most of the day compare with similar districts in our state?
  2. Does the share differ by disability category, grade level or school building?
  3. Do general education teachers have the co-teaching time, training and support staff that inclusion needs?
  4. Are any placements driven by where programs or staff happen to be, rather than by each student's IEP?

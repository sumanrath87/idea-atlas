What the law requires

Under IDEA, each state must examine its data every year to decide whether significant disproportionality based on race and ethnicity is happening in its districts (34 CFR 300.646). The review covers three areas:

  • Identification: students with disabilities overall, and in six categories: intellectual disability, specific learning disability, emotional disturbance, speech or language impairment, other health impairment, and autism.
  • Placement: students with IEPs who spend less than 40% of the day in a general class, or who learn in separate schools and residential facilities.
  • Discipline: suspensions and expulsions, in- and out-of-school removals, and their length.

When a state finds significant disproportionality in a district, the state reviews, and if needed requires the district to revise, its policies, practices and procedures. The district also sets aside 15% of its IDEA Part B funds for comprehensive coordinated early intervening services, which can reach children from age 3 through grade 12, with and without disabilities.

The arithmetic: the risk ratio

States use a risk ratio (34 CFR 300.647). It compares how likely students in one group are to experience something with how likely all other students in the district are. If 15 of every 100 students in one group have IEPs, and 8 of every 100 of all other students do, the risk ratio is 15 ÷ 8, or about 1.9.

Each state sets its own threshold, minimum group sizes, and number of years. A district identified in one state might not be identified in another with the same numbers.

What the national figures show

Nationally, these gaps are modest next to the thresholds states use. That is the point of looking locally. Disproportionality is decided district by district, and a national average can hide large differences in particular places and categories.

The atlas figures help you see patterns. They are not the state’s official finding. States use their own rules, and often the IDEA child count rather than civil rights data. A higher rate is a reason to ask how students come to be referred and identified. It does not on its own show that any student was identified incorrectly.

Reading it from the bottom up

When a district is identified, the required steps are a review, a plan and a set-aside of funds. Catherine Voulgarides (2018) found that staff in cited districts often treated showing compliance as the accomplishment, while the everyday practices behind the numbers continued. McCall and Skrtic (2009) describe disproportionality as a “wicked problem” that does not yield to a single technical fix.

Who decides which behaviors lead to a referral, and do those decisions get looked at together across classrooms?

Questions to ask

  • Was our district identified with significant disproportionality in recent years? If so, in which area, and what did the review find?
  • If the district set aside funds for early intervening services, what were they used for, and did anything change?
  • What happens before a referral for evaluation? Which supports does a student get in the general class first?
  • When the district reviewed its practices, did it talk with the students and families most affected?

Look up your district

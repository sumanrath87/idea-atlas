## The options

Most disagreements are settled in conversation, at an IEP meeting or with a principal or special education director. When that does not work, IDEA provides formal routes.

| Option | Who decides | Federal timeline |
|---|---|---|
| **State complaint** | The state education agency investigates | A written decision within 60 days of the complaint, with extensions only in limited cases (34 CFR 300.152) |
| **Mediation** | The family and school, with a neutral mediator | No set timeline; voluntary for both sides, free to the family, and any agreement is in writing and binding (34 CFR 300.506) |
| **Due process complaint** | A hearing officer, if the case goes to a hearing | A resolution meeting within 15 days, a 30-day resolution period, then a hearing decision within 45 days (34 CFR 300.510, 300.515) |

**A state complaint** can be filed by anyone, not only a parent, about a violation of IDEA in the past year. It is often used for problems like services in an IEP not being delivered (34 CFR 300.151–300.153).

**Mediation** is available at any time, including before or instead of a due process hearing. It is confidential, and the state pays for it.

**A due process complaint** asks for a hearing about a child's identification, evaluation, placement, or free appropriate public education. It must be filed within two years of when the family knew or should have known about the problem, unless the state sets a different limit (34 CFR 300.507). Most due process complaints never reach a hearing: they are settled in the resolution period, in mediation, withdrawn, or dismissed.

## Reading your state's counts

Each [state page]({base}states/) shows four counts from the federal dispute resolution data:

- signed, written state complaints
- **mediation requests**
- **due process complaints**
- due process hearings fully held

These counts are hard to compare across states. Large states have more disputes because they have more students. Some states settle more problems informally before a complaint is ever filed. A state with very few complaints may have few problems, or families who do not know how to file. Comparing a state with itself over time tells you more than ranking states.

Two federal indicators use these data. [Indicator 15]({base}guides/indicators/15-resolution-sessions/) reports the share of resolution meetings that ended in a written settlement, and [indicator 16]({base}guides/indicators/16-mediation/) reports the share of mediations that ended in an agreement.

## Questions to ask

- Before filing anything: have I asked, in writing, for an IEP meeting to discuss the concern?
- Would mediation let us keep working together while settling this one issue?
- Who in my state can help? Every state has at least one federally funded [Parent Training and Information Center](https://www.parentcenterhub.org/find-your-center/) that helps families for free.
- How many complaints and hearings does my state have compared with a few years ago?

## Beyond compliance

IDEA's dispute options are rights, and families should know them. They are also a sign of what happens when collaboration breaks down. A due process hearing is a legal contest. It can fix one child's program and leave the school's way of working untouched.

Skrtic (1991b) argued that schools serve students with disabilities best when staff work as collaborative problem-solvers, adjusting their practice together and with the people they serve. Kozleski and Thorius (2014) make the case for involving families in school improvement all along, before anything goes wrong. Questions that follow:

- What happens after a complaint is resolved? Does the school change anything for other students?
- Are families asked how to prevent disputes, not only how to settle them?
- Does the district track what disputes are about, and share what it learns?

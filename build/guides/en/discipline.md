## Where the numbers come from

Discipline figures in the atlas come from the Civil Rights Data Collection (CRDC), which the U.S. Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights gathers from nearly every public school. The atlas uses the 2023–24 collection.

For each group of students, the atlas shows how many of every 100 students in that group:

- received one or more out-of-school suspensions
- were expelled
- were physically restrained or mechanically restrained
- were secluded
- were referred to law enforcement, or had a school-related arrest

The groups are students with IEPs, students with Section 504 plans, and students without disabilities. Each rate uses its own group as the base, so you can compare a group of 300 with a group of 3,000.

## Reading a suspension comparison

A district page might say:

> In 2023–24, 4.5 of every 100 students with IEPs received an out-of-school suspension, compared with 1.7 of every 100 students without disabilities.

That means a student with an IEP in this district was about two and a half times as likely to be suspended out of school at least once. The figure counts students, not suspensions, so a student suspended five times counts once.

Before drawing a conclusion, check two things under "Full detail": how many students the rates stand for, and whether any figures were withheld for privacy. Restraint and seclusion counts in particular are often small, and a single student can change them a great deal.

## What IDEA requires

Students with IEPs can be disciplined, but IDEA adds protections once removals add up.

- **Short removals.** A school may remove a student with a disability for up to 10 school days in a row for breaking a code of conduct, as it would any student (34 CFR 300.530(b)).
- **After 10 days in a school year**, the school must keep providing services during any further removal, so the student can keep working toward IEP goals (34 CFR 300.530(b)(2), (d)).
- **A change of placement** happens when a removal lasts more than 10 school days in a row, or when a series of shorter removals adds up to more than 10 days and forms a pattern (34 CFR 300.536).
- **Manifestation determination.** Within 10 school days of deciding on a change of placement, the district, the parents, and relevant IEP team members must decide whether the behavior was caused by, or had a direct and substantial relationship to, the disability, or was the direct result of the district not carrying out the IEP (34 CFR 300.530(e)).
- If it was, the team must do a functional behavioral assessment and put in place, or review, a behavioral intervention plan, and the student generally returns to the prior placement (34 CFR 300.530(f)).
- Special rules allow removal to an interim setting for up to 45 school days for weapons, drugs, or serious bodily injury, whatever the manifestation finding (34 CFR 300.530(g)).

Many districts also send students home early or ask families to pick them up. These "informal removals" often do not appear in suspension counts, but they can still count as removals under IDEA.

## How the federal indicators use discipline data

States report on discipline in [federal indicator 4]({base}guides/indicators/4-suspension-and-expulsion/). It looks at suspensions and expulsions of more than 10 days in a school year and asks whether any districts show a significant discrepancy, overall and by race and ethnicity. States also check discipline data for significant disproportionality by race and ethnicity, which is explained in [its own guide]({base}guides/disproportionality/).

## Questions to ask

- How many school days has my child missed because of removals this year, including early pickups and partial days?
- Has the team done a functional behavioral assessment? Is there a behavior plan, and is it being followed?
- How does the district's suspension rate for students with IEPs compare with students without disabilities, and with the state?
- What does the district do instead of suspension, and is it available at every school?

## Beyond compliance

IDEA's discipline rules are mostly timelines and procedures: count the days, hold the meeting, write the plan. Following them matters. It also leaves room for what Skrtic called ceremonial compliance, where the required steps are done and practice stays the same (Skrtic, 2005). Catherine Voulgarides found this pattern in districts cited for discipline disparities: staff worked hard to show compliance, and the conditions that led to the suspensions changed little (Voulgarides, 2018).

Kozleski and Smith (2009) found that the stories adults tell about children shape how reforms play out in a school. Questions worth raising:

- When a student is removed, who decides what the behavior means, and are the student and family part of that conversation?
- What does the school change about the classroom or the day, not only about the student?
- Do students who were suspended get a say in what would have helped?

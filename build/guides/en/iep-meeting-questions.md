## Your place at the table

IDEA names the parents as members of the IEP team (34 CFR 300.321). The school must tell you about the meeting early enough for you to attend, and schedule it at a time and place you both agree on (34 CFR 300.322). You can bring someone who knows your child, such as a relative, a friend, an advocate, or a staff member from your state's Parent Training and Information Center. If you need an interpreter, the school must arrange one so you can follow the meeting (34 CFR 300.322).

A few rights are worth knowing before you go:

- You can look at your child's education records, and the school must let you do so without unnecessary delay, and before any IEP meeting (34 CFR 300.613).
- If the school proposes or refuses a change, it must give you prior written notice explaining what it plans and why (34 CFR 300.503).
- If you disagree with the school's evaluation, you can ask for an independent educational evaluation at public expense (34 CFR 300.502).
- The IEP must be reviewed at least once a year, and your child must be reevaluated at least every three years unless you and the school agree it is not needed (34 CFR 300.324, 300.303).

## Before the meeting

- Can I see the draft IEP, evaluation reports, and progress data a few days ahead?
- Who will be at the meeting, and what role does each person have?
- What does my child say about school? Could my child take part in all or part of the meeting?
- What do our district's figures show? Look up your district in the atlas and note one figure that raises a question for you, such as time in general classes or suspensions.

## About goals

The Supreme Court has said an IEP must be reasonably calculated to help a child make progress appropriate in light of the child's circumstances, and that the child's program must be "appropriately ambitious" (*Endrew F. v. Douglas County School District*, 2017).

- What will my child be able to do in a year that they cannot do now?
- How was each goal chosen, and how does it connect to grade-level work?
- How will progress be measured, and how often will I get a progress report?
- If my child did not meet last year's goals, what will be different this year?

## About services and setting

- What services will my child receive, how often, for how long, and from whom?
- What supports will help my child learn in the general class, and what has been tried?
- If my child will spend time outside the general class, what is that time for, and how will we know when it is no longer needed?
- Will my child take part in lunch, recess, clubs, and field trips with other students?

## About behavior and discipline

- Does my child's behavior get in the way of learning? Has the team done a functional behavioral assessment?
- Is there a behavior plan, and who makes sure every adult follows it?
- Has my child been sent home early or removed from class this year? Removals count, even informal ones.

## About life after school

Starting no later than the IEP in effect when your child turns 16, or younger if the team decides it is appropriate, the IEP must include measurable goals for life after high school and the transition services to reach them (34 CFR 300.320(b)). Some states start earlier.

- What does my child want to do after high school, and how did the team find out?
- Which courses, work experiences, and community connections are in the plan?
- Which adult agencies should be invited, and when?

## After the meeting

- When will I get a copy of the final IEP?
- Who do I contact if a service is not happening?
- If I do not agree with the IEP, what are my options? (See [complaints, mediation, and due process]({base}guides/dispute-resolution/).)

Write down what was agreed and who will do what. A short email to the team summarizing the meeting gives everyone the same record.

## Beyond compliance

An IEP meeting can be run entirely by the book and still leave a family feeling like a visitor. Skrtic (2005) pointed to IEPs and IEP meetings as examples of how schools can meet the letter of the law through rituals that do not change practice. Kozleski and Thorius (2014) argue that schools improve when students and families help shape the work from the start.

Questions that go past the paperwork:

- Did anyone ask my child what helps them learn, and is it in the IEP?
- Is this plan written for my child, or copied from a menu of standard services?
- After the meeting, will the people who work with my child every day actually see and use the IEP?

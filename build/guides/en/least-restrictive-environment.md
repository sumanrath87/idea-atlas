## What the law requires

The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) requires that, to the maximum extent appropriate, children with disabilities are educated with children who do not have disabilities. Special classes, separate schools, or other removal from the general class may happen only when the nature or severity of the disability means that education in general classes, with supplementary aids and services, cannot be achieved satisfactorily (34 CFR 300.114).

Three other rules sit next to that one:

- Every district must have a continuum of placements available, from general classes to special classes, special schools, home instruction, and instruction in hospitals and institutions (34 CFR 300.115).
- **Placement is decided by a group that includes the parents**, at least once a year, based on the child's IEP. Unless the IEP requires something else, the child attends the school they would attend if they did not have a disability, and placement should be as close as possible to home (34 CFR 300.116).
- Students with disabilities also take part with other students in meals, recess, and other nonacademic activities to the maximum extent appropriate (34 CFR 300.117).

The law does not set a number. It sets a starting point, the general class with supports, and asks the team to justify any move away from it for each child.

## How the federal data measure it

Each fall, districts report how much of the school day each student with an IEP, ages 5 (in kindergarten) through 21, spends inside a general class. Students fall into one of these groups:

| Setting | What it means |
|---|---|
| 80% or more of the day in a general class | Most of the day with classmates, sometimes with support in the room or short times elsewhere |
| 40% to 79% | Part of the day in a general class |
| Less than 40% | Most of the day in a special class |
| Separate school | A school only for students with disabilities |
| Residential facility | A school where students also live |
| Home or hospital | Instruction at home or in a hospital |

The atlas shows the first three as rates per 100 students with IEPs, plus the separate settings combined. These same figures feed federal indicator 5, which every state reports each year.

## Reading your district's figure

On any district page, open "Where students learn." Suppose you see this for a New Jersey district:

> About 52 of every 100 students with IEPs spend 80% or more of the day in a general class.

Before deciding whether that is high or low, compare it with the right neighbors. In 2024–25 the typical New Jersey district reported 52.1, and the middle half of New Jersey districts fell between 40.5 and 63.8. Nationwide, the typical district reported 74.7. So this district is ordinary for New Jersey, and New Jersey as a whole sits well below most of the country. Both facts matter, and they lead to different questions. You can see your own state's figures on its [state page]({base}states/).

Three cautions:

- **Who is in the district changes the figure.** A district that runs a regional program for students with significant support needs will show more students in special classes.
- **Separate schools may not show up where you expect.** Students a district places in a separate school or in another district's program can be counted in different ways from state to state, so read the "Full detail" counts.
- **Small districts move a lot.** In a district with 30 students with IEPs, one student is more than 3 of every 100.

## What the numbers leave out

Time in a room is not the same as being part of the class. The federal figure cannot tell you whether a student in a general class for 80% of the day has the supports to learn there, takes part in discussion and group work, has friends, or is following the same curriculum. Two districts with the same figure can offer very different experiences.

The figure also says nothing about preschool. Children ages 3 to 5 are measured separately, in [federal indicator 6]({base}guides/indicators/6-preschool-environments/).

## Questions to ask

- For my child: what supports would make more time in the general class work, and what has the team tried?
- If time is spent outside the general class, what is the goal of that time, and how will the team know when it is no longer needed?
- How does the district's figure compare with its state, and does the district know why?
- Are students in special classes taking part in lunch, recess, clubs, and field trips with everyone else?

## Beyond compliance

Thomas Skrtic described special education as one way that schools, organized around standard programs, handle students who do not fit those programs: by moving them somewhere else (Skrtic, 1991a). On that view, a low figure is less about individual students and more about how the school is organized, and more paperwork will not change it. Skrtic argued for schools that work as collaborative problem-solving teams instead (Skrtic, 1991b).

Dianne Ferguson, Elizabeth Kozleski, and Anne Smith treated inclusive schooling as a change to the whole school rather than a question of where some students sit (Ferguson, Kozleski, & Smith, 2001). Questions that follow from both:

- When a student struggles in the general class, does the school change the class, or move the student?
- Do general and special education teachers plan together, with time set aside for it?
- Have students who spend most of the day in special classes been asked where they want to learn, and what would help?

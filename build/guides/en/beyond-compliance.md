## Why I built a data tool and then wrote this

I built IDEA Atlas so that anyone could look up a school district and see, in plain words, how it serves students with disabilities. Every figure in it comes from a federal report that a district or state is required to file. That is its strength: the data exist everywhere, in the same form, every year.

It is also a limit. My research is about inclusive education, policy, and change that starts inside schools and communities. Again and again, the questions that matter most to students and families are ones no federal form asks. This essay is about that gap, and about what it would take to work past it.

## How compliance became the measure

When Congress reauthorized IDEA in 2004, it required every state to write a State Performance Plan and report on it each year. Those reports now cover 18 indicators. Some measure results, such as graduation, time in general classes, and what young people do after high school. Others measure compliance: whether evaluations happened within 60 days, whether IEPs were in place by a child's third birthday, whether transition plans had every required part. For compliance indicators, the target is 100%, or 0% for the ones that count problems.

For several years, the U.S. Department of Education's yearly ratings of states rested on compliance alone. In 2014 it changed course. Its new approach, Results Driven Accountability, moved the weight of federal oversight away from compliance alone and toward results for students. The change was a federal admission of something families and teachers already knew: a state can meet nearly every procedural requirement while students with disabilities make little progress.

The courts have said something similar about individual students. In 2017 the Supreme Court held that an IEP must be reasonably calculated to help a child make progress appropriate in light of the child's circumstances, and that a child's program must be "appropriately ambitious" (*Endrew F.*, 2017). A plan can be written on time, signed by the right people, and still fall short of that.

## What compliance can hide

Thomas Skrtic has spent decades asking why schools find it so hard to change for students who do not fit. His answer is about how schools are organized. Most schools work like what organizational theorists call professional bureaucracies: each teacher applies a standard set of practices to the students in front of them. That works well for students the standard practices fit. Students who do not fit have to go somewhere else, and special education became that somewhere (Skrtic, 1991a).

When outside mandates arrive, Skrtic argued, these organizations do not simply change. They respond by signaling compliance through what he called "symbols and ceremonies of change" that stay largely disconnected from daily practice, and he named IEPs and IEP meetings as examples (Skrtic, 2005). Catherine Voulgarides later watched this happen in districts cited by their states: staff worked hard to document compliance, and documenting it became the accomplishment, while the conditions behind the citations continued (Voulgarides, 2018).

None of this means the rules are wrong. The procedures in IDEA exist because, before 1975, many children with disabilities were turned away from school altogether. Timelines and safeguards protect families who would otherwise have no leverage. The point is narrower: compliance tells you a process happened. It does not tell you what the process did for a child.

## What going further looks like

Skrtic's alternative to the professional bureaucracy is what he called an adhocracy: a school organized around collaborative problem-solving, where staff adjust their practice together, and with the people they serve, when a student's needs do not fit the usual approach (Skrtic, 1991b). Today we might call it a learning organization, or a community of practice.

Elizabeth Kozleski's research on school change adds two things. First, change happens at several levels at once: classrooms, schools, districts, and states have to move together, or the change does not last (Ferguson, Kozleski, & Smith, 2001). Second, structures are not enough. Kozleski and Smith (2009) found that reforms take hold, or fail to, through the stories a school community tells about its children. Kozleski and Thorius (2014) argue for putting students and families at the center of improvement.

Put together, these ideas suggest some practical habits:

- **Read the numbers with the people they describe.** A district's suspension figure means something different when students who were suspended help explain it.
- **Treat every compliance figure as a floor.** A timely evaluation is required. A useful one is the goal.
- **Change the classroom before moving the child.** When a student struggles, ask first what the class and the school could do differently.
- **Give teachers time to solve problems together.** Collaboration that depends on lunch breaks will not hold.
- **Ask what the data cannot see.** Belonging, friendships, a student's own goals, and a family's sense of being heard are not in any federal file. They can still be asked about.

## How to use this alongside the atlas

Every guide in this series ends with a short section called "Beyond compliance." Each one takes a federal figure and asks the questions that the figure cannot answer. Use them at an IEP meeting, a school board meeting, a parent group, or a staff meeting. Start with the numbers. Then talk with students and families. The second step is where change begins.

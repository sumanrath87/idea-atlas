## What the law requires

Under IDEA, each state must collect and examine data every year to decide whether significant disproportionality based on race and ethnicity is happening in the state and its districts (34 CFR 300.646). The review covers three areas:

- **Identification:** students with disabilities overall, and in six disability categories: intellectual disability, specific learning disability, emotional disturbance, speech or language impairment, other health impairment, and autism.
- **Placement:** students with IEPs who spend less than 40% of the day in a general class, or who learn in separate schools and residential facilities.
- **Discipline:** suspensions and expulsions, in- and out-of-school removals, and their length.

When a state finds significant disproportionality in a district, three things must follow (34 CFR 300.646(c)–(d)):

1. The state reviews, and if needed requires the district to revise, its policies, practices, and procedures, and the district reports any revisions publicly.
2. The district sets aside 15% of its IDEA Part B funds for comprehensive coordinated early intervening services, to find and address the factors behind the disproportionality.
3. Those services can reach children from age 3 through grade 12, with and without disabilities.

## The arithmetic: the risk ratio

States use a risk ratio (34 CFR 300.647). It compares how likely students in one group are to experience something with how likely all other students in the district are.

Here is a worked example with made-up numbers. In a district, 30 of 200 students in one group have IEPs, a risk of 15 in 100. Among all other students, 120 of 1,500 have IEPs, a risk of 8 in 100. The risk ratio is 15 ÷ 8, or about 1.9. Students in that group are about 1.9 times as likely to have an IEP.

Each state sets its own rules for applying the ratio:

- a threshold, such as 3.0, above which the ratio counts as significant
- **minimum group sizes**, so a handful of students does not decide the result
- whether to require several years above the threshold before a district is identified
- whether a district making reasonable progress may be excused

Because these choices differ, a district identified in one state might not be identified in another with the same numbers.

## Related federal indicators

Three federal indicators look at nearby questions, using different methods:

- [Indicators 9 and 10]({base}guides/indicators/9-disproportionate-representation/) ask whether any district has disproportionate representation by race or ethnicity in special education, overall and by disability category, that results from inappropriate identification. The target for both is 0%.
- [Indicator 4B]({base}guides/indicators/4-suspension-and-expulsion/) asks whether any district has a significant discrepancy by race or ethnicity in long suspensions and expulsions of students with IEPs, and policies or practices that contribute to it and do not follow IDEA.

## Reading the atlas figures

District pages include a section, "By race and ethnicity," under "Who is served" and "Discipline and safety." It shows, for each group, students with IEPs per 100 students enrolled and out-of-school suspensions per 100 students with IEPs, from the 2023–24 Civil Rights Data Collection.

These figures help you see patterns. They are not the state's official finding:

- States use their own thresholds, group sizes, and years, and often the IDEA child count rather than civil rights data.
- Small groups produce unstable rates. The atlas leaves out cells that were withheld for privacy.
- A higher rate is a reason to ask how students come to be referred and identified. It does not on its own show that any student was identified incorrectly.

## Questions to ask

- Was our district identified with significant disproportionality in recent years? If so, in which area, and what did the review of policies and practices find?
- If the district set aside funds for early intervening services, what were they used for, and did anything change?
- What happens before a referral for evaluation? Which supports does a student get in the general class first?

## Beyond compliance

When a district is identified, the required steps are a review, a plan, and a set-aside of funds. Catherine Voulgarides studied districts cited for disproportionality and found that staff often treated showing compliance as the accomplishment, while the everyday practices behind the numbers continued (Voulgarides, 2018). McCall and Skrtic describe disproportionality as a "wicked problem" that does not yield to a single technical fix (McCall & Skrtic, 2009).

Kozleski and Smith (2009) found that how adults in a school community talk about children shapes how reforms take hold. Questions that follow:

- When the district reviewed its practices, did it talk with the students and families most affected?
- Who decides which behaviors lead to a referral or a suspension, and do those decisions get looked at together across classrooms?
- Is the review treated as a one-time task, or as an ongoing look at how the school works?

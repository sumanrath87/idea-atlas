## Start with who is counted

Most figures in the atlas are written as "per 100." Before you read one, check who the 100 are. The same district can have very different numbers depending on the group.

- **Students with IEPs per 100 enrolled** compares the students who have an Individualized Education Program with all students in the district.
- **Where students learn** and Leaving school count only students with IEPs. A figure of 60 there means 60 of every 100 students with IEPs, not 60 of every 100 students.
- **Discipline** figures are counted separately for each group: students with IEPs, students with Section 504 plans, and students without disabilities. That lets you compare the groups fairly even when one is much larger than another.

Each section on a district page says which group it counts, just under the heading. If two numbers seem not to fit together, the groups are usually the reason.

## Check the year

The atlas brings together several federal collections, and they do not all come from the same school year.

| What you are reading | School year |
|---|---|
| Students with IEPs, where they learn | 2024–25 |
| How students with IEPs left school | 2023–24 |
| Discipline, restraint, seclusion, Section 504 | 2023–24 |
| Spending and federal IDEA funds | Fiscal year 2024 |
| Child poverty | 2024 estimate |

A district that changed a lot in the last year or two may look different in these figures than it does today. The year is printed with every figure.

## Compare with the right neighbor

A single number rarely means much on its own. The atlas gives you two comparisons for most figures.

**The typical district in your state.** This is the median: line up every district in the state from lowest to highest, and it is the one in the middle. Rules and funding differ a great deal from state to state, so this is usually the fairest comparison.

**The middle half of districts in your state.** Open "The numbers" under any section to see the range where the middle half of districts fall. A district inside that range is not unusual for its state. A district far outside it may be worth a closer look.

You can also compare with districts of similar size and child poverty using "Districts like this one" at the bottom of each page, or pick districts yourself on the [Compare]({base}compare/) page.

## Watch for small numbers

In a district with 40 students with IEPs, one student is 2.5 of every 100. A change of three or four students can move a figure a long way. The atlas shows the counts behind every rate under "Full detail," so you can see how many students a figure stands for.

Federal agencies also hide some small counts to protect students' privacy. When you see "withheld for privacy" or "fewer than 10 students," the figure was not published, and the atlas does not guess it.

## Know what the numbers cannot tell you

These figures describe what districts reported to the federal government. They are useful, and they are limited.

- They are not grades. A high or low figure is a reason to ask a question, not an answer.
- They do not show quality. A district can report that most students with IEPs spend most of the day in a general class without telling you whether those students are learning there.
- They miss what students and families experience. No federal collection asks whether a student feels they belong.

That last point matters most. The numbers tell you where to look. Talking with students, families, and teachers tells you what is happening.

## Put it to use

1. Read the "At a glance" sentences first.
2. Open "The numbers" for one section you care about and compare with your state's typical district.
3. Check the count under "Full detail" before drawing a conclusion from a small district.
4. Write down one question the figure raises, and bring it to an IEP meeting, a school board meeting, or a conversation with the special education director.
5. If you want a printed version, use [Make a report]({base}report/) to get a report written for a family, a school leader, or a researcher.

## Beyond compliance

Every number on a district page exists because federal law requires someone to report it. That is useful, but it can quietly turn the numbers into the goal. Thomas Skrtic argued that schools often answer outside mandates with "symbols and ceremonies of change" that stay disconnected from what happens in classrooms (Skrtic, 2005). A district can report every figure on time and still change very little for students.

Elizabeth Kozleski's work on school change points another way. Kozleski and Smith (2009) found that reforms play out through the stories a school community tells about its children, not only through new structures. Kozleski and Thorius (2014) argue for putting students and families at the center of school improvement. Some questions to bring to the numbers:

- Who in this district looks at these figures, and do families and students ever look at them with staff?
- When a figure is unusual for the state, what story does the district tell about it, and who else might tell a different one?
- What would we want to know about students' experience that no federal figure measures, and how could we find out?
